    <h2> Work in Progress </h2>
    <ul>
      <li> <b><span style="color:rgb(2, 48, 71);">Job Market Paper: </span>Not Just Weather: How Climate Change Reduces Incomes and Limits Mobility</b> </li>
      <details>
            <summary><u>Abstract.</u></summary>
            <p>Climate change is heterogeneous across regions, and the resulting welfare losses hinge on workers’ ability to relocate. It is widely held that when climate change decreases incomes in one area, affected populations will migrate toward less exposed regions. Drawing on internal migration data from Brazil, I show that negative income shocks indeed spur out-migration, but that this response is significantly dampened where access to credit is limited. This empirical pattern is consistent with a theoretical framework in which incomplete credit markets, fixed migration costs, and diminishing marginal utility amplify the effective burden of moving for poorer households, reducing their mobility. I then show that fixed migration costs & liquidity constraints have important implications for the interplay between incomes and mobility. Indeed, if climate change reduces incomes, migration costs (in terms of foregone utility) increase, and migration flows decline rather than increase, intensifying welfare losses for vulnerable populations. To quantify these dynamics, I develop a multi-region, multi-sector general equilibrium model of the Brazilian economy with region-specific income distributions and dynamic migration decisions. Using climate predictions, I then use the model to simulate the economy from 2010 to 2100.</p>
        </details>
      <li> <b>Skill Supply, Firm Size, and Economic Development</b>, with <a href="https://sites.google.com/site/gottliebcharles/">Charles Gottlieb</a> and <a href="https://markus-poschke.research.mcgill.ca/">Markus Poschke</a>. </li>
        <details>
            <summary><u>Abstract.</u></summary>
            <p>Across countries, the organisation of production differs widely, and firms are substantially smaller in low-income countries. How are these differences related to a country’s skill endowment? This paper studies the interplay between skill endowment and firm size along the development path. Empirically, we measure the skill intensity of employment by firm size for 57 countries and document four facts. First, we show that the share of employment in large firms is about four times as high in high-income countries as in low-income countries. Second, we find that across countries, employees of large firms are more skilled than those of small firms. Third, whereas small firms in rich countries are almost as skill-intensive as large firms, small firms employ many fewer skilled workers in poor countries. Fourth, whereas small firms are nearly as skill-intensive as large firms when the skill premium is low, they are much less skill-intensive when the skill premium is high. This evidence suggests that small firms can easily substitute low-skill for high-skill workers when high-skill workers are scarce and expensive, but large firms are less flexible. As a result, the low skill endowment of low-income countries limits the size of firms in these countries. We then use a span-of-control model with worker skill heterogeneity and two technologies (large and small scale) to analyze the effect of skill endowments on the firm size distribution and economic development. Calibrated to the US and varying only skill endowments, our model closely replicates skill intensity by firm size across countries. It implies that, to a large extent, the lower skill endowments of poor countries lie behind the four facts we document. Our findings also suggest that greater skill levels benefit production directly and through a shift to large-scale production units. Finally, we illustrate how our work relates to the literature on misallocation.</p>
        </details>

      <li> <b>Connectivity and Comprehension: Infrastructure, language, and the making of a common market</b>, with <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/rolandhodler">Roland Hodler</a> and <a href="https://paulschaudt.com/">Paul Schaudt</a>. </li>
              <details>
            <summary><u>Abstract.</u></summary>
            <p>In this paper, we estimate how physical and non-physical intra-regional trade barriers have evolved within Switzerland over time. Our analysis documents long-term trends in mode-specific transportation costs and quantifies the evolution of linguistic frictions, revealing that improvements in both areas have substantially enhanced market access. Using a counterfactual framework, we estimate that if language barriers had persisted at their 1888 levels, Switzerland's market connectivity would have been significantly diminished, even if infrastructure improvements had occurred. Turning to mechanisms, we document that the expansion of the infrastructure network has driven the increase in market access during the 1890 to 1920 period, after which technological progress has decreased trade costs on roads. Moreover, we document that linguistic frictions fall while primary and secondary education access is broadened and other domestic languages become mandatory subjects in school. In summary, our findings highlight the critical interplay between infrastructure development and education policy to reduce both physcial and nonphyscial trade barriers within countries, ultimatly fostering economic development.</p>
